\subsection{Local Noise-Floor Adaptive Spectral Thresholding}
Load changes the SES floor in both directions. At $0$~HP the rolling elements
press lightly on the raceways. The impacts are weak, and a fixed threshold set
for normal running sits above the fault peaks, so they are missed. At $3$~HP
friction raises the whole floor, and the same threshold is crossed by noise in
dozens of bins. No single fixed value satisfies both cases.

A CFAR rule~\cite{richards} handles both cases. It slides a window along the SES and estimates
the local floor from the bins around the test bin, so the comparison is always
made against the floor that is actually there. No training data is needed, and
the false-alarm rate stays fixed as the load changes.

For a test bin $k$, the $N_G$ bins on each side are kept out of the estimate.
The Hann mainlobe is four bins wide, so $N_G=4$ is enough to stop a fault peak
from entering its own estimate. The $N_R=25$ bins beyond each guard band form
the reference window
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathcal{W}_k=\big\{\text{SES}[m]\;\big|\;&
  m\in[k-N_G-N_R,\,k-N_G-1]\\
  &\cup[k+N_G+1,\,k+N_G+N_R]\big\}.
\end{aligned}
  \label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
Two estimators are compared on $\mathcal{W}_k$.

\subsubsection{Cell-Averaging CFAR}
Cell averaging takes the mean of the window, as a moving-average filter would:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mu}[k]=\frac{1}{2N_R}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{W}_k}x,
  \qquad T_{\text{CFAR}}[k]=\alpha\,\hat{\mu}[k],
  \label{eq:cacfar}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=3.5$. This works while the floor changes slowly. It fails when a
harmonic enters the window. A shaft harmonic at $1\times$ or $2\times$ RPM, or
a nearby fault harmonic, pulls the mean up, and the threshold rises beside the
fault, which is the one place it should stay low. A mean is easy to move: one
large bin is enough. This is harmonic target masking, and it is the reason the
next estimator exists.

\subsubsection{Proposed Order-Statistic Median--MAD Threshold}
The proposed rule uses order statistics instead of the mean:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \hat{m}[k] &= \operatorname{median}\left(\mathcal{W}_k\right),\\
  \text{MAD}[k] &= \operatorname{median}\left(\left|\mathcal{W}_k-\hat{m}[k]\right|\right).
\end{aligned}
  \label{eq:medmad}
\end{equation}
The factor $1.4826$ is the standardized nominal scale that makes the MAD
agree with the standard deviation for a normal distribution,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\sigma}_{\text{local}}[k]=1.4826\,\text{MAD}[k],
  \label{eq:madscale}
\end{equation}
and it is used here as a fixed scale factor rather than as an unbiased
estimate of a Gaussian $\sigma$, because the SES floor is not Gaussian.
The threshold is
\begin{equation}
  T_{\text{MAD}}[k]=\hat{m}[k]+\gamma\left(1.4826\,\text{MAD}[k]\right),
  \label{eq:tmad}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma=7.0$. A larger $\gamma$ raises the threshold and lowers the
false-alarm rate; a smaller one does the opposite. The median has a breakdown
point of $50\%$~\cite{huber}, so up to half of the window can be contaminated before
$T_{\text{MAD}}$ moves. The threshold follows the floor from $0$~HP to
$3$~HP.

\subsubsection{Slip-Tolerant Harmonic Comb}
Heavy radial load also causes slip. The rolling elements skid by $1$--$2\%$,
so the measured fault frequency is not the kinematic value. The detector
therefore searches a band, not a single bin,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{B}_h=\left[hf_{\text{fault}}(1-\delta_{\text{slip}}),\;
  hf_{\text{fault}}(1+\delta_{\text{slip}})\right],
  \label{eq:comb}
\end{equation}
for each fault frequency, $f_{\text{BPFO}}$, $f_{\text{BPFI}}$ or
$f_{\text{BSF}}$, and each harmonic order $h=1,2,3$. The half-width is
$\delta_{\text{slip}}=1.5\%$, narrow enough to exclude the neighbouring
harmonic orders and wide enough to cover the slip. A harmonic is present when
\begin{equation}
  \max_{f_k\in\mathcal{B}_h}\left(\text{SES}[k]-T_{\text{MAD}}[k]\right) > 0.
  \label{eq:decision}
\end{equation}

The exceedance rate that this rule produces is not a thermal-noise
false-alarm probability. On a bearing SES the eligible non-harmonic bins
contain unmasked shaft harmonics and modulation sidebands, so the measured
rate counts the discrete interference lines that the harmonic mask did not
cover; that is the quantity that matters for a detector operating on this
kind of spectrum.
